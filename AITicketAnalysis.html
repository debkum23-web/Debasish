01

Problem

We were sitting on 18 months of support tickets, more than 1,200, and the roadmap was still being driven by whoever complained loudest. Reading it all by hand would have taken 4–6 weeks, so it went unread.

The cost was twofold. Much of the support demand traced back to avoidable dependency on engineering, and roadmap conversations were debates between opinions with nothing to settle them.

02

Product Decision

I decided to let ticket volume settle prioritization, and to redirect investment toward self-service and automation, where the tickets pointed, instead of towards engineering-dependent fixes.

01

Volume over opinion

Ticket counts, not who complained loudest, decide which features get a build slot.

02

AI finds the pattern, I make the call

Copilot did the classification. Every AI-generated category still got a manual spot-check, and the decision on what to build stayed mine.

03

Saying no to ten to say yes to two

The analysis surfaced 12 theme clusters. Only two had the volume and a clear enough scope to justify building.

03

Stakeholder Alignment

Turning an unstructured backlog into a number a skeptic can't easily argue with was the alignment strategy. “310 tickets are asking for this” ended prioritization debates that “I think users want this” never could.

Before anything went in front of stakeholders, every AI-generated category had been spot-checked by hand, so nobody was being asked to trust a model's output. When I proposed Copy Site Pages I could point to 310 tickets, and Re-boarding Automation had 185 behind it. The roadmap conversation changed from debating anecdotes to reading counts.

04

Technical Approach

From raw tickets to shipped features

I used Excel Copilot to classify and theme the raw ticket descriptions — 65% feature requests, 25% defects, 10% enhancements — and cluster them into 12 product areas. Copilot did the pattern-finding; a manual spot-check gated every category before I relied on it.

Two of the actual prompts I used

Analyze the Description column in the Support_Tickets table and create a new Issue Category column grouping similar user problems.
Using the Issue Category column, identify trends and highlight issues that show sustained growth over time.

Time investment

A manual read-through of 18 months of tickets would have taken 4–6 weeks. The Copilot-assisted pass took 6–8 hours plus validation time.

05

Outcome

30%

Fewer support tickets, as investment moved toward self-service and automation instead of engineering-dependent fixes.

2 shipped

Copy Site Pages (310 tickets) and Re-boarding Automation (185 tickets), both built on ticket volume, not opinion.

80%

Cut in manual ticket-review time: 4–6 weeks of reading down to a 6–8 hour Copilot pass plus validation.

What each feature delivered

  • Copy Site Pages Utility (internal tool, not public): built from the 310-ticket signal, it cut page-cloning time from hours to minutes.
  • User Re-boarding Access Automation: built from the 185-ticket signal, it cut access resolution from 3–5 days to minutes.
06

Ongoing Ownership

This wasn't a one-off analysis. It replaced opinion-driven prioritization with a data-driven process, and that process stayed with the roadmap as I moved from refining the backlog to owning it end to end.

The rule I kept is the same one that ran the first pass: AI surfaces the pattern, the evidence has to be checked before it's trusted, and the decision on what gets built stays with a person.
